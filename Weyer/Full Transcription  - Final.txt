**A Letter of Doctor Johann Wier, touching certaine Creatures which doe proceed from the Imagination**

*To the Reverend and Learned Doctor M. H.,*

Right Worshipfull Sir,

Your last letter hath given me occasion to consider againe a matter which I had heretofore esteemed of little moment, namely, whether those formes and creatures which doe arise in the Imagination of Man may have any being or understanding apart from the minde which first conceiveth them. I confesse unto you, I was at the beginning much disposed to reject the whole matter as a phantasie, and nothing more. Yet certaine observations have caused me to moderate that judgement; for I have once conversed with one such creature in the waking world, and have received many other accounts from persons of sober judgement, besides certaine dreames which, though they cannot serve as proof, have left impressions not easily dismissed. You know that I have written concerning divers apparitions, illusions, witchcrafts, and other matters commonly accounted supernatural, and that I have oft maintained that men are too ready to attribute unto spirits that which may proceed from the distemperature of the minde. In this present matter, however, I have been brought unto a position which I did not expect.

My first acquaintance with these matters was through the report of a person who affirmed that he had repeatedly seen a certaine figure, and who maintained that this figure had been formed originally in his owne Imagination. I suspected, as any physician ought, that some disorder of the senses or minde might be the cause. I therefore examined the man carefully concerning his health, his sleepe, his passions, his feares, and the occasions upon which the figure appeared. I caused him to describe the creature at length, and compared his account with those of others who had encountered it. To my surprise, the creature was not confined unto his sight. Other persons could perceive it, speake unto it, and observe its actions, even when the first man was not present. This caused me to enquire more diligently whether the creature might be no more than a common error amongst those who had heard the same tale; yet I found that some witnesses had no knowledge of the earlier accounts, and that their descriptions nevertheless agreed in particulars which I should not readily have expected them to invent together.

Most of what I know concerning these beings hath come unto me by such reports. Some were delivered by the persons who first conceived them; others by neighbours, servants, travellers, or physicians who had no reason, so far as I could discerne, to agree upon a falsehood. Their accounts differ in many particulars, as accounts of strange things commonly doe, yet certain features recur with such persistence that I cannot dismiss them all as the effects of melancholy or deceit. I have also dreamed of several of these creatures, though I make a strict distinction between what hath been reported unto me and what hath appeared only in sleepe. Dreames may reveal the workings of the minde, and may sometimes shew us formes which have been impressed upon us without our knowledge; but they doe not by themselves establish the existence of anything beyond the minde. Nevertheless, when the same creature is described in waking encounters and afterwards appeareth in dreames unto persons who have never seen it, I think the matter worthy of further enquiry.

One creature in particular hath occupied my thoughts more than the rest, being a small deer of an unusual pinke colour, concerning which I have received accounts from persons who, so far as I can discover, had no communication one with another before their several encounters. The creature is not, as one might suppose from the name alone, altogether like unto a natural deer. It hath certain human proportions and manners, and is said to stand upright upon two legs rather than upon four. Its forelegs or forefeet are used in much the same manner as a man's hands, and it is capable of taking hold of things, gesturing, and performing other actions which no natural deer would perform. It is likewise said to understand speech and to answer when spoken unto. In this respect it is more properly described as a deer in forme and appearance, but partly manlike in its body and behaviour. I note these particulars because they recur in the several accounts, although the witnesses had not, so far as I can determine, received them from one another.

I have been especially struck by the agreement betwixt these reports. In all of them the creature is described as pleasing and comely in its outward forme, of a gentle countenance and manner, and possessed of that sort of prettinesse which commonly stirreth affection toward a young or harmless beast. Yet in almost every account there is likewise a second impression, difficult for the witnesses to explain, whereby the creature proveth disquieting or even fearful to behold. They cannot ordinarily say what it is in the creature which troubleth them. Its eyes are not described as evil, nor its teeth or manner as threatening; it doth not commonly attack, nor make any overt shew of malice. Rather, the uneasinesse seemeth to arise from the whole presence of it, as though something in its manner were not altogether as it should be, though the observer cannot name the defect. This is the more remarkable because its humanlike forme ought, by ordinary expectation, to make it less strange to us, yet the contrary seemeth often to occur.

One person described the creature as *a faire little beast, gentle in countenance and manner, yet strangely troubling to behold*; another called it *a pretty and harmless deer, though with somewhat in the looke thereof which made me unwilling to draw too near*. A third, who had never heard either of these descriptions, used words of much the same meaning after seeing it in a dreame. Even the dreames, although they cannot serve as evidence of an outward creature, have possessed a remarkable agreement in this respect: the deer is not presented as a monster, nor as a thing plainly hostile, but rather as a creature which inviteth affection whilst at the same time awakening an uneasinesse for which the dreamer can give no sufficient cause. I have myselfe dreamed of this deer, and was troubled afterwards by the degree to which my own impression corresponded unto these several accounts. I take this resemblance of reports with caution, for dreames are easily shaped by what a man hath heard beforehand; yet I had not expected so many witnesses, in waking and sleeping alike, to agree upon the same peculiar mixture of prettinesse and dread.

What is yet more remarkable is that the pinke deer doth seeme, according to the accounts, to retain a consistent manner of behaviour from one encounter unto another. It is quiet, attentive, and watchfull, and seemeth more inclined to observe the person before it than to flee from him. It may approach with a gentlenesse which would ordinarily invite the hand, yet those who have attempted to draw near unto it report a certaine inward hesitation, as though some part of their minde warned them against treating it as an ordinary beast. Its standing posture, humanlike use of its forefeet, and manner of regarding those around it give unto the creature a resemblance unto Man which is difficult to reconcile with the forme of a deer. Yet neither is it simply a man disguised in the shape of a beast. There remaineth something distinctly cervine in its countenance and carriage, so that the eye perceiveth both kinds together without being able to reconcile them. Whether this contradiction of formes be the cause of the uneasinesse which so many witnesses describe, I cannot say; indeed, most of them are unable to name it themselves. Nevertheless, when such agreement is found amongst persons separated by time and circumstance, I think it proper for a physician to take notice of it, even where he cannot yet account for it.

Of the creatures themselves I have had but one personal encounter which I dare call certaine. It was brief, and I shall not pretend that it resolved the questions which have arisen from it. The creature stood before me in a chamber where another person had previously described it, though the person who first imagined it was not present. It answered when spoken unto, turned its head toward sounds, and displayed a manner of attention which seemed more than the mechanical repetition of an Image. I observed its posture and movements, and found them to be neither wholly those of a man nor wholly those of a beast. It seemed capable of using its forefeet in a manner resembling hands, and its manner of regarding me was unmistakably attentive. I did not examine it closely, nor did I attempt to touch it, for I confess that I was uncertain whether such an experiment would have been wise. Its appearance was strange, but not monstrous; indeed, there was something in its forme which might readily have moved a man unto affection, had there not been at the same time that obscure uneasinesse which hath been reported by others. After a short while it departed, and I have not since seen it with my waking eyes.

Yet the reports of others describe creatures which continue long after their first appearance. I have compared the accounts of those who first conceived them with the behaviour attributed unto the creatures themselves, and I have found between the two a resemblance which is remarkable, though by no means perfect. Their bodies, faces, voices, manners, and inclinations commonly bear some relation unto the Image from which they proceeded, much as a child resembleth the parent from whom it descendeth. Yet the resemblance hath its limits. A creature may possess the shape and habits of a man, and yet its understanding may be unlike that of any natural man I have known. A beast may have the outward forme of a deer, horse, or hound, yet there may be some inward disposition which is not that of the natural beast, but rather a likeness of the notion which Man hath formed concerning such an animal.

I know not what name may properly be given unto this difference. They reason, remember, deliberate, desire, feare, and take offence; yet there is in all these operations something which seemeth to me strangely foreign. They possess the outward forme of human understanding, but I am uncertain whether the inward substance thereof be the same. They may be called rational, but their reason doth not alwayes proceed by the same paths as ours. They may desire friendship, dominion, knowledge, or revenge, but the causes of these desires are sometimes obscure. It is as though the Imagination had fashioned not merely the appearance of a man, but a likeness of his inward life, and yet had made that inward life according unto some rule which we doe not comprehend. Thus I have sometimes wondered whether these beings ought to be regarded as having a kinde of understanding which is neither ours nor wholly unlike ours, but rather a counterfeit or similitude of our own understanding, made according unto laws which remain hidden from us.

I have therefore made it my business to enquire whether their thoughts correspond unto those of the persons from whose Imagination they proceeded. Here the matter became still more curious. According to the reports, the creature doth not simply repeat the thoughts of its maker. It may disagree with him, surprise him, or act contrary unto his wishes. It possesseth memories which its maker doth not claim to have given it, and it may speake of matters which were never consciously conceived by him. In certaine cases the creature seemeth even to have forgotten things which its maker remembereth clearly. Thus I cannot regard it as a mere Image continually governed by the minde which first conceived it. Whatever its origin may be, it seemeth afterwards to possess a certaine independence.

This independence hath made me cautious in using the word *phantasie*. A phantasie commonly signifieth something which hath no being save in the minde of him who imagineth it. These creatures, however, are said to continue when the minde which first conceived them is occupied with other things, and they may be observed by persons who know nothing of their supposed origin. The one creature which I myselfe encountered was not present merely as a thought in the minde of its maker, for that person was elsewhere and knew nothing of my seeing it. Nor have I received any trustworthy account that such a creature simply ceaseth to be when its maker ceaseth to think upon it. Rather, the evidence thus far suggesteth the contrary: that once brought forth, it may continue in some fashion of existence apart from the continuing attention of the person whose Imagination first gave it forme. Whether this existence be natural, spiritual, or of some other order which we have not yet named, I cannot determine.

More difficult still is the question of their bodies. The accounts I have received describe some as exceedingly strange when closely considered, though at a distance they may appear no different from the bodies of ordinary men or beasts. Others are so exact in their resemblance that no ordinary examination would distinguish them from their natural counterparts. Yet in certain cases their bodies may suffer injuries which are not merely apparent.

One witness reported having seen such a creature wounded, and affirmed that blood issued from the wound, fell upon the ground, and stained the cloth beneath it. The creature itself, he said, exhibited pain and fear, withdrew from the person who had wounded it, and afterwards guarded the injured place with considerable care. I cannot vouch for every part of this account, yet it was repeated by another person who had observed the creature afterwards. The wound was said to resemble a natural wound in many particulars, though the loss of blood did not weaken the creature in precisely the manner expected. It was neither wholly natural nor wholly illusory, but seemed in some manner to imitate the one while remaining the other. This distinction is difficult to express, yet I think it important. For if the blood be merely an Image, how doth it stain the cloth? And if the wound be truly bodily, by what nature is the body constituted?

I have also received an account of the death of one such creature. It had the forme of a man and had been known unto several persons for some time. After receiving a grievous injury, it fell and ceased to answer. Its breathing diminished, and after a short while there was no sign of life. The witnesses remained near it and examined the body afterwards. There was no sudden vanishing, nor did it dissolve like a dreame or an apparition. It remained where it had fallen, and for all appearances was a dead body. Indeed, had they encountered it without knowing its origin, they would have taken it for the corpse of a natural man. What became of it afterwards I cannot say, for the accounts cease at that point. I have received other reports of creatures being slain, and these likewise describe the death as a real event rather than the mere cessation of a vision.

This much I may say: these creatures are not merely fleeting Images which disappear when their maker ceaseth to think upon them. They may continue their existence independently, if the reports be trusted, and the death of the creature doth not appear to be equivalent to the mere cessation of the thought which first produced it. Whether their existence commenceth entirely within the minde and afterwards passeth into some other condition, or whether the Imagination serveth only as a means whereby something else is brought forth, I cannot determine. I am not yet satisfied that the creature is *made* in the same manner that a man maketh an Image upon parchment. It may rather be that the Imagination provideth the forme, whilst some other principle provideth the substance.

This hath led me unto another question which I confesse doth greatly occupy my thoughts. If these creatures possess an understanding resembling our own, and if they possess an Imagination of their owne, can they likewise bring forth such creatures by imagining them? I have asked those who claim to have spoken with them whether the creatures themselves ever described such an act. Their answers have been uncertain and contradictory. Some say that the creatures can imagine almost anything, whilst others maintain that their Imagination is of a different kinde from ours and cannot give being unto that which it conceiveth. I have not yet been able to determine the truth of this.

I am especially curious concerning whether a creature born of Imagination may itself become a maker, or whether there is some natural limit placed upon these beings which preventeth them from bringing forth others of their kinde. If they cannot doe so, I should much desire to know why. If they can, the consequence would be exceedingly difficult to comprehend, for we should then have creatures which owe their first existence unto the Imagination of Man, yet which possess the power of producing other beings whose origin is likewise imaginative. Such a matter would require far more careful study than I have yet been able to give it. It might even be necessary to reconsider what we mean when we distinguish between the creator and the creature, for the creature would then possess, in some measure, the same faculty whereby it itselfe came into being.

I have made certain enquiries in this direction, but I would not call them experiments until they have been repeated under better conditions. I have asked witnesses to describe what the creatures themselves claimed concerning their powers, and I have recorded several dreames in which such beings appeared to attempt the making of others. Yet speech is not proof, and dreames are less than proof. A man may describe the Philosopher's Stone without possessing it, and so may an imaginary creature speake of making another creature without being capable of the act. For this reason I remain doubtful, and I would rather preserve the question than supply it with an answer which the evidence doth not warrant.

There is also a further reason for my reluctance to publish these observations. You know that I have not been silent upon occult matters when I have believed that silence would permit error or cruelty to flourish. Yet I have learned that the common reader hath little patience for uncertainty. If one writeth that a strange thing hath been observed, there will presently be men who repeat that it hath been proved. If one writeth that an apparition may have a natural cause, there will be others who declare that one hath denied the existence of spirits altogether. The presse is therefore a poor place for an observation which remaineth so far from being understood.

In this present case I have an additional feare. Should it become commonly known that certain creatures proceeding from Imagination may acquire an independent existence, that they may possess understanding, and that some may bleed or even be killed, I greatly doubt whether men would be content merely to study them. Some would seeke to summon them, others to command them, and others perhaps to devise means of making creatures of their owne. I have no wish to provide instruction unto such persons, nor to encourage the credulity of those who are already disposed to see spirits and devils in every unusual occurrence. Worse still, there are men who, being cruel by nature, might take pleasure in discovering whether such creatures can suffer pain. I would not willingly furnish such persons with a new object for their cruelty.

For this cause I entrust these matters unto you, and unto a very small number of other persons whose discretion I have had occasion to prove. Perhaps, after more observation and more exact enquiry, I shall find that the matter admitteth of a simple explanation. Perhaps I shall discover that some of what hath been reported proceedeth from the disorders of those who first conceived these creatures, and that only a portion of the phenomenon is truly extraordinary. Yet until I know more, I think it better to keep my counsel amongst trusted associates than to expose the matter unto the multitude. I have no desire to establish a new doctrine where I possess only observations, nor to make a spectacle of creatures whose nature I myselfe cannot yet comprehend.

I would not have you mistake my caution for disbelief. I am not persuaded that these creatures are spirits, nor am I persuaded that they are merely phantasies. They seeme to occupy some condition between the two, being born, so far as we can determine, from the Imagination, yet thereafter behaving in a manner which the Imagination of their maker doth not wholly govern. Their bodies may imitate those of natural creatures, their passions may imitate ours, and their reason may resemble ours, yet in each of these there is some difference which I cannot presently name. They are like unto us in the same manner that a likeness in a looking-glasse is like unto the man before it, except that these likenesses may speake, remember, desire, suffer, and continue when the man hath departed.

Whether they possess soules I shall not presume to say. Whether they possess a proper Imagination, capable of producing anything beyond the thoughts which arise within themselves, remaineth likewise uncertain. I intend to make further enquiry concerning this point, but shall proceed with caution, for I have already seen enough to know that the things which are most easily imagined by men are not alwayes the things which are most easily understood. Perhaps the greatest error would be to suppose that because their beginning is in the Imagination, their nature must therefore be wholly imaginary.

I pray you therefore keepe this letter private, and shew it only unto such persons as you know to be sober in judgement and unwilling to turn every uncertainty into a marvel. If further observations should establish anything worthy of publication, I shall consider whether the time hath come to make it known. Until then, I am content that these strange creatures should remain a subject of private enquiry amongst a few learned friends, rather than become another occasion for the credulity and superstition of the multitude. If I have written more freely unto you than I should unto the presse, it is because I trust you to distinguish betwixt that which I have seen, that which others have reported, and that which I merely suspect.

God keepe you in health and grant you wisdom in all your studies.

Your loving friend and servant,

**Johann Wier, Doctor of Physicke**
